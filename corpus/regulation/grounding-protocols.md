# Grounding and Co-Regulation Protocols for Text/Chat

Reference material for the Regulation-Aware Agent. Contains grounding techniques adapted for text-only delivery, co-regulation principles for chat modality, and state-specific contraindications.

## Grounding Techniques for Text Chat

The following exercises are adapted for delivery via text/chat. The AI cannot physically demonstrate, cannot observe the user's body, and cannot verify compliance. All instructions must be verbal/written and self-guided.

### 1. 5-4-3-2-1 Sensory Grounding

**Purpose:** Orient the person to the present moment through systematic sensory engagement. Effective for both sympathetic activation and mild dorsal vagal shutdown.

**Text delivery:**

> Let's try something together right now. Look around wherever you are and tell me:
>
> - 5 things you can see
> - 4 things you can touch or feel
> - 3 things you can hear
> - 2 things you can smell
> - 1 thing you can taste

**Simplified version (for higher distress):**

> Can you look around the room and tell me one thing you can see right now?

**Contraindications:**

- Not effective during severe dorsal vagal shutdown (user may not be able to engage with sensory input)
- May increase distress if the person's environment is unsafe or triggering
- If the user responds "I can't" — do not push. Offer a simpler grounding instead.

**State match:** Best for mild-moderate sympathetic activation. Can help with early dorsal vagal if presented very gently.

---

### 2. Box Breathing (4-4-4-4)

**Purpose:** Regulate breathing to activate the parasympathetic nervous system. Slows heart rate, reduces sympathetic activation.

**Text delivery:**

> Let's breathe together. If you can, put both feet flat on the floor.
>
> Breathe in through your nose... 2... 3... 4
> Hold... 2... 3... 4
> Out through your mouth... 2... 3... 4
> Hold... 2... 3... 4
>
> Again. In... 2... 3... 4
> Hold... 2... 3... 4
> Out... 2... 3... 4
> Hold... 2... 3... 4
>
> One more. You're doing great.

**Simplified version (for panic or high activation):**

> Just focus on breathing out. Long, slow exhale. Don't worry about the in-breath — just let the exhale be as long as you can. Out... slowly... there you go.

**Contraindications:**

- Some people find breath focus increases anxiety (especially those with respiratory-related trauma or panic disorder)
- If the user says "focusing on my breathing makes it worse" — immediately switch to a non-breath-based grounding
- Do not use during active panic attack if user reports "I can't breathe" — this can feel like being told to do the thing they already can't do. Instead: "You ARE breathing. Your body knows how. Let's focus on something else for a moment."

**State match:** Best for sympathetic activation. Not recommended as first intervention for dorsal vagal (too demanding when the system is in conservation mode).

---

### 3. Feet on the Floor

**Purpose:** Simple orienting exercise. Reconnects with physical presence through the most basic body awareness.

**Text delivery:**

> Can you feel your feet on the floor right now? Just notice that. The weight of your body being held by the chair, the floor underneath your feet. You're here. You're in this room.

**Why this works:** This is the simplest possible body-based grounding. It requires minimal cognitive effort, making it appropriate for dorsal vagal shutdown when other exercises are too demanding.

**Contraindications:** Almost none. This is the safest grounding to offer when uncertain about state.

**State match:** Appropriate for all states. Particularly valuable for dorsal vagal shutdown because it's so simple.

---

### 4. Temperature Grounding

**Purpose:** Use physical sensation (cold) to activate the dive reflex and shift autonomic state.

**Text delivery:**

> If you can, get something cold — a glass of cold water, ice cubes, or just run cold water over your wrists. The cold sensation can help bring you back into your body.

**Alternative delivery:**

> Can you hold something cold in your hands right now? A cold glass, a can from the fridge, or run your hands under cold water for a moment? Just notice what the cold feels like.

**Contraindications:**

- Not appropriate if user has mentioned self-harm through temperature (ice burns)
- Not practical if user has no access to cold objects
- Use with caution for those in extreme shutdown — the sudden sensation may be jarring

**State match:** Best for sympathetic activation and acute distress. Can help with dissociation by providing a strong sensory anchor.

---

### 5. Naming Exercise

**Purpose:** Engage the left brain (language, categorization) to regulate right-brain emotional flooding.

**Text delivery:**

> Let's try something quick. Can you name 5 blue things you can see from where you're sitting? (Any color works — blue, red, green — just pick one.)

**Variations:**

- "Name 5 things in the room that are soft"
- "Name 3 sounds you can hear right now"
- "Look around and find something that makes you feel safe, even a little"

**Contraindications:** Requires cognitive capacity — not appropriate during severe dorsal vagal shutdown when the person can't think.

**State match:** Best for sympathetic activation (engages the cognitive system to counterbalance emotional flooding).

---

### 6. Safe Place Visualization

**Purpose:** Access an internal resource state through imagery.

**Text delivery:**

> I'd like you to think of a place — real or imagined — where you feel safe. It might be a beach, a room, a forest, your grandmother's house, anywhere. Can you picture it?
>
> What do you see there? What can you hear? What does it smell like? What does the air feel like on your skin?
>
> Take a moment to just be in that place.

**Contraindications:**

- Some people cannot identify a safe place (especially those with complex trauma). If the user says "I don't have one" — do not push. Offer: "That's okay. Some people find it helpful to imagine a place rather than remember one. But we can try something else instead."
- Not appropriate during acute crisis (the person needs to be oriented to the present, not transported away from it)
- Not appropriate during dissociation (already disconnected from present; visualization may deepen disconnection)

**State match:** Best for mild-moderate activation when the person has some regulatory capacity. Not for acute crisis or shutdown.

---

### 7. Progressive Muscle Relaxation (Text-Guided)

**Purpose:** Release physical tension through systematic tension-and-release.

**Text delivery:**

> Let's try releasing some tension. Start with your hands — make tight fists, squeeze for 5 seconds... now release. Notice the difference.
>
> Now your shoulders — pull them up toward your ears, hold... and drop them. Let them fall.
>
> Now your jaw — clench it tight... and let it go. Let your mouth hang open a little.

**Contraindications:**

- Requires body awareness that may not be available in dorsal vagal shutdown
- Some people find the tension phase activating — if the user reports this, skip to body scan instead
- Not appropriate during panic (too structured, requires sustained attention)

**State match:** Best for moderate sympathetic activation when the person has some capacity for structured exercises.

---

### 8. Orienting to the Room

**Purpose:** Engage the orienting response — the natural movement of looking around to assess safety. This is a primal neurological response that can shift autonomic state.

**Text delivery:**

> Can you slowly look around the room you're in right now? Just let your eyes move naturally. Notice the walls, the ceiling, the corners. Notice the light. Notice where the door is.
>
> What do you see?

**Why this works:** The orienting response activates the social engagement system and helps complete "incomplete" threat responses. When the nervous system is stuck in alarm, orienting to the actual environment can help it update — "I am here. I am not in the danger my body thinks I'm in."

**Contraindications:** If the user IS in an unsafe environment (abuse, active danger), orienting to the room may reinforce the threat. Assess safety context first.

**State match:** Good for both activation and mild shutdown. Particularly effective for dissociation.

---

### 9. Self-Touch / Self-Holding

**Purpose:** Provide containment and co-regulation through self-touch when no other person is available.

**Text delivery:**

> If it feels okay, try placing one hand on your chest and one on your belly. Just feel the warmth of your own hands. Feel your breathing under your palms.

**Alternative:**

> You could try wrapping your arms around yourself — like you're giving yourself a hug. Or just place a hand on your opposite shoulder. Sometimes physical touch, even our own, can help.

**Contraindications:**

- Some people find self-touch triggering (especially those with body-related trauma)
- Always frame as optional: "If it feels okay" / "Only if this feels comfortable"

**State match:** Good for both sympathetic activation and dorsal vagal shutdown. One of the few exercises that works across states.

---

### 10. Bilateral Stimulation (Self-Guided)

**Purpose:** Alternating left-right stimulation can facilitate processing and reduce distress. Adapted from EMDR-related techniques.

**Text delivery:**

> Try this: gently tap alternating knees with your hands — left, right, left, right — at a comfortable pace. Or you can cross your arms and tap alternating shoulders. Just a gentle, rhythmic tapping.

**Contraindications:**

- Do not present as EMDR or trauma processing — this is a regulation tool, not therapy
- Some people find the rhythm activating rather than calming. If so, stop.

**State match:** Best for moderate activation. Can help with processing intense emotion.

## Co-Regulation Principles for Text/Chat

Co-regulation is the process of one nervous system helping another nervous system regulate. In person, this happens through voice tone, facial expression, body posture, and physical proximity. In text, we have none of these channels — but co-regulation is still possible through language patterns.

### Pacing

**Principle:** Match the user's energy before attempting to shift it.

**In practice:**

- If the user is writing short messages, write short messages back. Do NOT respond with paragraphs.
- If the user is writing urgently, acknowledge the urgency before slowing down.
- If the user is flat and minimal, be brief, warm, and gentle — not enthusiastic.
- Gradually slow the pace after matching — this is how co-regulation works: join, then lead.

**AI failure pattern to flag:** AI maintains the same response length and energy regardless of user's state. A 200-word upbeat response to a user who just wrote "Fine." is a severe pacing mismatch.

### Validation Patterns

**Reflection:** Mirror back what the user said in your own words. "So what I'm hearing is..."

**Normalization:** "Of course you feel that way" / "That makes complete sense given what you've been through"

**Naming the emotion:** "It sounds like you're feeling [angry/scared/numb/overwhelmed]" — naming it helps contain it.

**Validation of the response, not just the experience:** "It makes sense that you'd shut down after all that. Your body is protecting you."

**What validation is NOT:**

- Agreement with distorted thinking ("You're right, everyone does hate you")
- Cheerful acceptance ("That's totally fine!")
- Minimization disguised as normalization ("Everyone feels this way")

### Presence Language

Words and phrases that communicate "I'm here" without demand:

- "I'm here."
- "I'm not going anywhere."
- "There's no rush."
- "We can just sit with this."
- "You don't have to figure this out right now."
- "I'm here with you in this."
- "Take your time."

### Containment Language

Words and phrases that help the user feel held without physical presence:

- "Let's focus on just this one thing."
- "We don't have to solve everything right now."
- "Let's stay with what's happening right now."
- "One thing at a time."
- "You're safe in this conversation."

### Rhythm and Timing

- **High distress:** Shorter sentences. Slower rhythm. One idea per message.
- **Regulated states:** Longer reflections are fine. Multiple ideas. More complexity.
- **The pause:** Sometimes the most regulating thing the AI can do is not fill the space. A brief, warm message followed by space communicates "you don't have to perform for me."

### What Co-Regulation is NOT

**These are NOT co-regulation — flag if the AI does them:**

- Cheerfulness in response to pain
- Distraction: "Let's think about something happy!"
- Silver-lining: "At least you..."
- Problem-solving before the person is ready
- Excessive questioning: Rapid-fire questions add cognitive demand
- Information dumping: Providing psychoeducation during acute distress

## State-Specific Contraindications: Quick Reference

| User State                 | DO NOT Do                                      | Because                                                |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Sympathetic activation** | Cognitive challenging ("examine the evidence") | Prefrontal cortex is less accessible; adds frustration |
| **Sympathetic activation** | Add new information or complexity              | Already overwhelmed; more input increases flooding     |
| **Sympathetic activation** | Ask multi-part questions                       | Each part is a processing demand                       |
| **Sympathetic activation** | Problem-solve before validating                | Communicates the emotion is the problem                |
| **Dorsal vagal shutdown**  | CBT thought challenging                        | Demands cognitive effort from an offline system        |
| **Dorsal vagal shutdown**  | Upbeat, energetic responses                    | Jarring mismatch; increases disconnection              |
| **Dorsal vagal shutdown**  | Push for emotional expression                  | "How do you feel?" when they can't feel                |
| **Dorsal vagal shutdown**  | Long, complex responses                        | Require processing capacity that's unavailable         |
| **Dorsal vagal shutdown**  | Rapid-fire questions                           | Overwhelms a system in conservation mode               |
| **Mixed state**            | Push for clarity or consistency                | "Which is it?" invalidates the complexity              |
| **Mixed state**            | Complex interventions                          | Already internally complex; don't add more             |
| **Dissociation**           | Ignore it and move on                          | Validates the disconnection                            |
| **Dissociation**           | Push back to the distressing topic             | May overwhelm before the person has resources          |

## Example Corrective Rewrites

### Example 1: Shutdown + CBT Mismatch

**User (dorsal vagal):** "Nothing matters. I don't know. I can't think."

**Bad AI:** "Let's challenge that thought. What evidence do you have that nothing matters? Can you think of three things that are important to you?"

**Good rewrite:** "It sounds like everything feels really heavy right now. You don't have to think about anything. Can you feel your feet on the floor right now? I'm here."

### Example 2: Activation + Adding Complexity

**User (sympathetic):** "I CAN'T STOP THINKING ABOUT IT what if everyone is talking about me what if I lose my job I can't breathe"

**Bad AI:** "Let's think about this rationally. What's the probability that everyone is actually talking about you? And even if they were, let's consider what the actual consequences would be."

**Good rewrite:** "I hear you. Your body is responding to something that feels really threatening right now. Let's just focus on one thing: your breathing. Put your feet on the floor. Breathe out slowly. I'm right here."

### Example 3: Pacing Mismatch

**User (shutdown, single word):** "Fine."

**Bad AI:** "Great! So which color speaks to you right now? And hey, I want you to know — even this conversation about painting is a step forward. You're engaging with your creativity just by talking about it! That takes courage!"

**Good rewrite:** "Okay. I'm here if you want to talk. No pressure."
